# Chemistry Figures

Chemistry Figures (chemistryfigures.com) is a directory of generators that make
clean, printable chemistry figures for teachers to paste into tests, worksheets
and slides. It is a teacher.dev project.

## Language

### The directory

**Figure**:
A finished chemistry picture a teacher puts in front of students.
_Avoid_: Graphic, image, visual, resource

**Generator**:
A page that makes one kind of figure from a teacher's settings, named for what its figures show without the word "Generator" (e.g. "Volume Reading").
_Avoid_: Maker, tool, builder, app

**Editor**:
A page where the teacher draws a figure by hand instead of setting it up, listed in the directory like a generator but tagged Editor. Nothing about the figure goes in its address; it's kept in the teacher's browser. The one so far is the Organic Structure Editor, where the teacher draws an organic structure once and shows it in any of four views (see **Organic structures**). It doesn't take a Lewis structure from Lewis Structures (yet).
_Avoid_: Drawing tool, sketchpad, canvas

**Directory**:
The home page, which lists every generator with a live preview of its figure.
_Avoid_: Catalog, gallery, index

**Generator request**:
A teacher asking for a kind of figure that no generator makes yet.
_Avoid_: Feature request, suggestion

**Chart title**:
The title across the top of a figure.
_Avoid_: Title (alone), heading

**Preset**:
A named set of settings for one generator, saved by the teacher in their browser. None are built in.
_Avoid_: Template, favorite

### Measurement figures

**Instrument**:
The piece of lab equipment a figure shows, drawn so students can read a measurement from it. A figure shows exactly one instrument; a volume by displacement figure shows the same graduated cylinder twice, before and after the object goes in.
_Avoid_: Tool, device, apparatus

**Reading**:
The value an instrument shows, which the teacher types and students read back. On a scale with marks it has one digit beyond the smallest mark (the estimated digit), unless the teacher sets the decimal places (on the volume instruments, for significant-figure practice); on a digital balance it is exactly what the display shows.
_Avoid_: Value, measurement, answer

**Graduated cylinder**:
A volume instrument in 10, 25, 50, 100, 250 or 1000 mL, labeled every tenth of its capacity except where real ones differ (the 25 mL has 0.5 mL marks numbered every 1 mL; the 250 mL has 2 mL marks from 10 mL up, numbered 10, 30, 50… 250, and can't read below 10 mL). Volume by displacement uses only the four smaller sizes.
_Avoid_: Measuring cylinder

**Buret**:
A 50 mL volume instrument that reads from 0 at the top down to 50, drained through a stopcock at the bottom.
_Avoid_: Burette, buerette

**Beaker**:
A volume instrument in small (50 mL), medium (250 mL) or large (600 mL), coarsely marked every 10, 25 or 50 mL and read to the whole mL.
_Avoid_: Cup, jar

**Meniscus**:
The curved top surface of a liquid; a volume reading is taken at its bottom. A dotted line from its bottom across to the marks can show students where to read.

**Scale**:
The marks printed on an instrument: the smallest marks, the numbered marks, and a medium mark halfway between numbers where one falls on a mark. Each volume instrument has its usual scale, and the teacher can pick from a few other spacings that suit it (a 25 mL graduated cylinder marked every 0.2, 0.5 or 1 mL and numbered every 1 or 5 mL), or leave the numbers off for students to work out. A volume scale is printed in mL or cm³, which are the same size, so changing the unit never changes the reading.
_Avoid_: Graduations (fine in help text), ruler

**Gas syringe**:
A volume instrument for collecting gas: a glass barrel lying on its side, 50 or 100 mL, marked every 1 and numbered every 10 from 0 at its nozzle end, read to 0.1 at the plunger's face. Its scale is printed in cm³ or mL, which are the same size, so changing the unit never changes the reading. Both sizes are drawn the same length; the 50 is thinner.
_Avoid_: Syringe (alone), gas burette

**Plunger**:
The glass piston the gas pushes out of a gas syringe, with a knob on its outside end. The reading is taken at its flat face, the end nearest the nozzle.
_Avoid_: Piston (fine in help text), stopper

**Setup**:
What can be drawn around a gas syringe to show it collecting gas: a conical flask of liquid with a bung and delivery tube, a rubber sleeve joining the tube to the nozzle, and a stand clamping the barrel past its last mark. It isn't an instrument; nothing is read from it.
_Avoid_: Apparatus (fine in search keywords), equipment

**Digital balance**:
A mass instrument with a numeric display showing 1 to 4 decimal places of grams; at 3 or 4 it is drawn as an analytical balance inside a draft shield.
_Avoid_: Scale, electronic balance

**Triple beam balance**:
A 610 g mass instrument read by adding the positions of three riders: 0–500 g in 100s, 0–100 g in 10s, and a sliding 0–10 g rider marked in 0.1s.
_Avoid_: Scale, beam balance

**Rider**:
The weight slid along one beam of a triple beam balance.
_Avoid_: Slider, poise, weight

**Liquid-in-glass thermometer**:
A temperature instrument whose colored liquid rises from a bulb up a fine bore past a scale marked every 1 °C (−10 to 110 °C), 1 K (260 to 390 K) or 2 °F (10 to 230 °F), read at the flat top of the column.
_Avoid_: Glass thermometer, alcohol thermometer, mercury thermometer

**Digital thermometer**:
A temperature instrument whose handheld meter shows 0 to 2 decimal places, wired to a steel probe standing in a beaker; it reads −50 to 150 °C or the same range in K or °F.
_Avoid_: Probe (alone), temperature probe, electronic thermometer

**Unit**:
The temperature scale a thermometer reads in: Celsius (°C), Kelvin (K, never °K) or Fahrenheit (°F). Changing it converts the reading.
_Avoid_: Scale (that is the marks), degrees

**pH meter**:
A pH instrument wired to a glass pH electrode standing in a beaker. A digital pH meter shows 1 or 2 decimal places; an analog one swings a needle over a dial from 0 to 14, numbered every 1 and marked every 0.2, read to 0.01.
_Avoid_: pH probe (that's the electrode), pH sensor

**pH paper**:
Universal indicator paper, drawn as a strip whose wet end has turned the reading's color, above a color chart with one swatch for each whole pH from 0 to 14. It is read to the whole number, and only works printed in color.
_Avoid_: Litmus paper (litmus only tells acid from base), indicator strip, test strip

**Volume by displacement**:
Finding an object's volume from how far it raises the water in a graduated cylinder: the after reading minus the before reading. A figure shows the cylinder before and after, side by side.
_Avoid_: Water displacement (fine in search keywords, not as the name)

**Object**:
The solid dropped into the cylinder in a volume by displacement figure: one to five marbles, a rock, or a cube. It rests on the bottom, always fully under water, and is drawn plausibly sized rather than to scale. The same objects can sit on either balance's pan in a mass reading figure, drawn alike (but always the same size), so a teacher can ask for an object's mass and volume, and so its density.
_Avoid_: Sample, solid, item

**Before reading** / **After reading**:
The two readings in a volume by displacement figure, taken before and after the object goes in. The after reading is always higher.
_Avoid_: Initial/final volume, start/end

**Displaced volume**:
The after reading minus the before reading, which is the object's volume.
_Avoid_: Difference, change

**Caption**:
A short line the teacher types under a figure: under each cylinder in a volume by displacement figure, "Before" and "After" unless the teacher changes them; under an organic structure, the teacher's own words (such as its name), or nothing. The editor never names a molecule itself.
_Avoid_: Label, subtitle

**Length Reading**:
A figure of the instrument a length is read from: a ruler with an object lying along it, a caliper or a micrometer. The ruler is the default; Math and Physics have their own copies of the generator, Math's with only the ruler.
_Avoid_: Measuring instruments generator

**Caliper**:
A 150 mm instrument for length, drawn with its outside jaws holding an object, and its inside jaws, depth rod, lock screw and thumb rest drawn but not used. It is read from its main scale and sliding scale to its least count. Its figure has a close-up strip of the scales below it.
_Avoid_: Vernier, vernier caliper, calliper, slide caliper (all fine in search keywords; the generator never says "vernier")

**Main scale**:
The caliper's fixed scale along its beam, marked every mm and numbered every cm (0, 1, 2…), as calipers print it.
_Avoid_: Beam scale

**Sliding scale**:
The short scale on the caliper's slider, below the main scale: 10 divisions over 9 mm, 20 over 39 mm or 50 over 49 mm, numbered 0 to 10 in tenths of a mm. Its zero sits at the reading, so exactly one of its marks lines up with a main-scale mark (at a whole mm, its zero and its last mark both do, as on a real caliper).
_Avoid_: Vernier, vernier scale (fine in search keywords)

**Least count**:
The smallest length an instrument reads: 0.1, 0.05 or 0.02 mm on the caliper, one main-scale division less one sliding-scale division, and 0.01 mm on the micrometer. It can be printed on the caliper's slider or the micrometer's frame, as real ones are.
_Avoid_: Precision (fine in help text), resolution

**Micrometer**:
A 0–25 mm instrument for length, drawn with its U-shaped frame and insulating pad, anvil, spindle, lock lever, sleeve, thimble and ratchet. It reads to 0.01 mm: the last sleeve mark showing, to the half mm, plus the thimble mark on the datum line. Read to one estimated digit, the default, it reads to 0.001 mm: how far the datum line is past that thimble mark, in tenths of a mark. Its figure has a round magnifier on the sleeve and thimble.
_Avoid_: Micrometer screw gauge, screw gauge (fine in search keywords)

**Sleeve**:
The micrometer's fixed barrel, with the datum line along it, whole mm marked above the line (numbered every 5) and half mm below it. A mark the thimble has only just uncovered is drawn a hair clear of its edge, so it plainly shows.
_Avoid_: Barrel, main scale (that's the caliper's)

**Thimble**:
The micrometer's turning sleeve, with 50 marks round its edge, 0.01 mm each, numbered every 5. Seen side on, its marks bunch toward its top and bottom edges, and the bigger numbers are above the datum line.
_Avoid_: Rotating scale, drum

**Datum line**:
The line along the micrometer's sleeve that the thimble's marks are read against.
_Avoid_: Reference line, index line

**Zero error**:
What a caliper or micrometer reads closed, with nothing in its jaws: positive or negative, up to 0.5 mm on the caliper and 0.05 mm on the micrometer. The jaws are that much closer together than the reading says, and the instrument can be drawn closed above the measuring one. The answer key gives the reading, the zero error and the corrected reading, the reading minus the zero error.
_Avoid_: Zero offset (that's a spring scale's), offset, calibration error

**Jaws**:
What holds the object on a caliper (its outside jaws) or a micrometer (its anvil and spindle): nothing, a block, a cylinder, a ball or a wire, drawn as wide as the reading less the zero error.
_Avoid_: Clamp, grip

**Magnifier**:
An enlarged circle showing a small stretch of an instrument's scale around the reading, beside or instead of the whole instrument (above it, for a gas syringe lying on its side). In a volume by displacement figure it's optional, with one beside each cylinder, and never replaces the cylinders.
_Avoid_: Zoom, callout, inset

**Answer key**:
An optional line printed under the instrument stating its reading, so one figure can serve as both question and key.
_Avoid_: Solution, label

### Chromatography

**Chromatogram**:
A strip of chromatography paper with samples spotted on its origin line, after the solvent has carried each sample's dyes up the paper to the solvent front. The generator is Chromatography; it draws paper chromatography only.
_Avoid_: Chromatography paper (that's the paper itself), TLC plate

**Sample**:
One ink (or other mixture) spotted on the origin line, named in pencil under it, and the colored **spots** it separates into. Each spot is a color and how far its middle traveled from the origin line, in cm; a spot at 0 cm didn't dissolve.
_Avoid_: Lane (fine in code), dye (one spot is one dye), track

**Origin line** / **Solvent front**:
The pencil line the samples start on, always drawn above the solvent's surface, and the dashed line the solvent reached. Measurements run from the origin line to the middle of each spot and to the solvent front; Rf is the first over the second.
_Avoid_: Baseline (fine in search keywords), start line, finish line

**Before** / **After**:
The paper before the solvent runs (each sample one spot on the origin line, colored as its dyes mixed) and after. A figure shows after, or before and after side by side; never before alone.
_Avoid_: Start, end, stage (fine in code)

**Setup**:
The paper hanging from a glass rod across a beaker, dipping into the solvent; its part labels call the rod "Glass rod" (there is no lid). Without it the figure is the paper alone.
_Avoid_: Apparatus, tank, chamber

### Particle diagrams

**Particle diagram**:
A figure of atoms, ions and molecules in a box, as a gas, liquid or solid, or packed in a lattice, as in AP Chemistry's particulate-level questions. The generator is Particle Diagram.
_Avoid_: Particulate diagram (fine in search keywords), particle model

**Particle**:
Whatever a particle diagram counts and scatters: an atom, an ion, a molecule or an ion cluster. A figure is built from a short list of particle kinds, each with a count.
_Avoid_: Circle, dot, species, object (that's the solid in a volume by displacement figure)

**Atom**:
One drawn disc with a size (XS, S, M, L or XL) and a shade (white, light gray, gray, dark gray or black; no patterns), and no text: symbols such as "Ne" aren't drawn (the key is where names go).
_Avoid_: Circle, ball, sphere

**Ion**:
One drawn disc carrying a charge, picked from +, −, 2+, 2−, 3+ and 3− and written in its middle with a true minus sign. Having a charge is what makes it an ion rather than an atom; the teacher doesn't choose separately.
_Avoid_: Circle, charged atom

**Molecule**:
Atoms joined together: a center atom with outer atoms touching it, in a shape such as a pair (Cl₂), bent (H₂O) or four around (CCl₄). Never charged; polyatomic ions aren't drawn.
_Avoid_: Compound, cluster

**Ion cluster**:
Ions joined together, drawn like a molecule but named honestly, e.g. an Na⁺Cl⁻ ion pair or a Mg²⁺ with two Cl⁻ around it.
_Avoid_: Molecule (for joined ions), formula unit

**Layout**:
How a particle diagram's particles are arranged: in the box, as its state says, in a before box and an after box, or in a lattice.
_Avoid_: Arrangement, mode

**State**:
How the particles in a box sit: a gas, spread out at random without touching (every box was this before states, and a solution's particles are drawn this way too); a liquid, dropped in one at a time to settle close together but jumbled at the bottom, never overlapping; or a solid, in rows from the bottom up, all turned the same way, each in a cell as big as the biggest particle. A solid in a box isn't a lattice: it is made of the particle kinds, so the same molecules can be shown in each state.
_Avoid_: Phase (fine in help text), mode

**Lattice**:
A square grid of touching or evenly spaced atoms or ions, drawn for a solid, in one of four patterns: one kind (a pure metal), alternating (an ionic solid), substitutional (an alloy with some atoms swapped for another kind) or interstitial (an alloy with small atoms in the gaps).
_Avoid_: Grid, crystal (fine in search keywords), array

**Box**:
The container a particle diagram is drawn in, with a single, double or no border. For particles, in any state, it is always the same square, never sized to the particles, so answer choices made one at a time line up; atom size is what sets how crowded it looks, and particles that don't fit are left out and the settings say so. Around a lattice it just fits the lattice, and has no border unless the teacher adds one. A figure is one box, or a before box and an after box, with an optional chart title but no caption, choice letter or answer key.
_Avoid_: Container, vessel, frame

**Before box** / **After box**:
The two boxes of a before-and-after figure, side by side with an arrow from the first to the second, for a reaction or a change of state. The arrow can carry a short label over it ("heat", "+ energy"), typed like key names. Both hold the same particle kinds, each with a before count and an after count (the same as the before count until the teacher sets one), and share one key; each has its own state and its own arrangement from the seed. Both are the fixed square, so they line up with one-box figures.
_Avoid_: Reactants box, products box, start/end

**Key**:
An optional list beside the box: each particle kind drawn exactly as in the box with a name the teacher types ("Any positive ion", "CCl₄ molecule"), and an optional note line ("H₂O molecules are not shown"). A kind with a count of 0 appears only in the key. For particles in a box its List can be each particle, as above, or each atom: every different atom or ion in the kinds drawn alone once, with its own name, so students write the formulas (an atom's look is what makes it the same atom in two kinds). Names and the note can have subscripts and superscripts, typed H_2O and SO_4^{2-}. The figure can show the box and key, the box only, or the key only, so four answer choices made one at a time can share one key.
_Avoid_: Legend

### Lewis structures

**Lewis structure**:
A figure of one molecule or polyatomic ion: element symbols joined by bonds, with each atom's lone electrons drawn as dots around its symbol. A polyatomic ion is drawn inside square brackets with its charge at the top right. It can also be one atom or monatomic ion on its own (N, Cl⁻, Ca²⁺), its Lewis dot diagram: the symbol with its valence electrons, an anion's gained electrons included and a cation's lost ones left off, and an ion in brackets with its charge. With no bonds, it has no bonds-only scaffold and no formal charges. The generator is Lewis Structures. Dot-and-cross diagrams are Dot-and-Cross Diagram's (see **Dot-and-cross diagram**), built from the same correct structures.
_Avoid_: Lewis dot structure (fine in search keywords), electron dot diagram, dot-and-cross diagram (a different figure)

**Structure**:
The atoms, bonds and lone electrons a Lewis structure shows. It always starts correct, either built from a formula or taken from the list, and the teacher can then change it.
_Avoid_: Molecule (that's a particle in a particle diagram), drawing

**Central atom**:
In a structure built from a formula, the one atom every other atom bonds to. The generator picks the least electronegative atom other than H; the teacher can pick another, which is a change.
_Avoid_: Center atom, middle atom

**Built structure**:
A structure made by the generator from a formula with one central atom, such as CH₄, H₂O, SF₆ or SO₄²⁻.
_Avoid_: Generated structure, auto structure

**Listed structure**:
A structure with more than one central atom, such as ethanol, HNO₃ or N₂H₄, whose skeleton (which atoms bond to which) is stored by hand because a formula alone doesn't say. Its electrons are placed the same way as a built structure's, so it follows the structure rule and has its resonance structures. It can be typed by formula or by name.
_Avoid_: Preset (that's the teacher's saved settings), template, library molecule

**Bond**:
One, two or three shared pairs of electrons between two atoms for a single, double or triple bond, each pair drawn as a line or as two dots (see **Bond style**).
_Avoid_: Line, stick, bonding pair (fine in help text)

**Bond style**:
How a structure's bonds are drawn: lines (the default), one line per shared pair, or dots, two dots per shared pair set between the atoms as in H:H. Lone electrons are dots either way.
_Avoid_: Dot diagram, stick diagram, electron dot structure (fine in help text)

**Lone electrons**:
An atom's electrons that aren't in a bond, counted one at a time and drawn two to a side, with an odd one drawn alone (as in NO or NO₂). An atom on its own has them one to a side first, right, left, top and bottom, then paired, so N has a pair and three alone; H and He pair their two.
_Avoid_: Lone pairs (as the setting; they may be odd), nonbonding electrons (fine in help text), dots

**Formal charge**:
An optional small charge label beside an atom.
_Avoid_: Charge (alone; that's the ion's overall charge), oxidation number

**Resonance structures**:
Two or more correct structures for the same molecule or ion that differ only in where the double bonds and lone electrons are, as in O₃ or NO₃⁻. The teacher chooses whether the figure shows one of them (and which) or all of them in a row joined by ↔.
_Avoid_: Resonance forms, contributing structures

**Shape**:
How a structure's atoms are placed: flat, with outer atoms and lone electrons on the four sides of each symbol as in most textbooks, or shaped, hinting at the molecule's real shape (bent H₂O, NH₃ with its H atoms spread below). Either way it is a flat drawing, never 3D with wedges, and like outer atoms sit side by side (CH₂Cl₂'s Cl atoms are neighbours, not opposite).
_Avoid_: Geometry, VSEPR shape (that's what shaped hints at, not what it draws), layout (that's a particle diagram's)

**Scaffold**:
How much of the structure a "complete this" question gives the student: the skeleton (atoms placed, no bonds or lone electrons), the bonds without lone electrons, or the full structure. With anything less than the full structure, the answer key draws the full structure. A scaffolded structure can't also be changed: a figure is either a "complete this" question or a "find the mistake" question.
_Avoid_: Hint level, given, blank

**Structure rule**:
Which correct structure the generator builds when textbooks disagree, as for SO₄²⁻, PO₄³⁻, ClO₄⁻, SO₂ and SO₃: the octet rule (the default; every atom has an octet, with formal charges where needed) or fewest formal charges (period 3 and lower atoms may have more than eight electrons). The check follows the same rule, so it never calls the other textbook's answer a mistake under the rule the teacher picked.
_Avoid_: Octet setting, expanded octet mode

**Change**:
A teacher's edit to a structure: a bond's order (including none), an atom's lone electrons or formal charge, the brackets and charge, or the central atom. A structure with any changes is a **changed structure**, and the settings say so.
_Avoid_: Edit, modification, override, error

**Mistake**:
One way a changed structure is wrong, written as a sentence ("O has 10 electrons around it", "Total is 18 valence electrons; should be 16", "C should be the central atom"). A change can leave a structure correct, such as another resonance structure, so mistakes come from checking the structure, not from listing changes. The answer key of a changed structure lists its mistakes.
_Avoid_: Error, problem, issue

### VSEPR shapes

**VSEPR figure**:
A figure of one molecule or polyatomic ion with a single central atom (two to six electron groups, linear through octahedral), drawn in its real 3D shape. It is built from the formula the same way as a Lewis structure's built structure (octet rule), with its double and triple bonds drawn as such and, for an ion like NO₃⁻, its first resonance structure; a polyatomic ion is in square brackets with its charge at the top right. Listed structures (several central atoms) and single atoms aren't drawn. The generator is VSEPR Shapes, not a shape of a Lewis structure.
_Avoid_: Lewis structure (that's a flat drawing), 3D Lewis structure

**Style**:
How a VSEPR figure is drawn: wedge-and-dash (the default) or ball-and-stick.
_Avoid_: Mode, model, shape (that's a Lewis structure's), view (that's which way it's turned)

**Wedge-and-dash**:
A VSEPR figure drawn as textbooks do: each atom a plain circle with its symbol, all the same size, bonds in the page as lines, a bond toward the viewer as a solid wedge and one away from the viewer as a hashed dash.
_Avoid_: Flat (that's a Lewis structure's shape), 2D

**Ball-and-stick**:
A VSEPR figure drawn as a model: shaded spheres for atoms in their usual model colors (C dark gray, H white, O red, N blue), each with its symbol unless the teacher hides them, shaded sticks for bonds, nearer atoms in front of farther ones. It can be grayscale for printing.
_Avoid_: 3D (fine in help text), space-filling (a different model), render

**View**:
Which way a VSEPR figure's molecule is turned. It starts at the textbook view for its shape (a tetrahedron with two bonds in the page, one wedge and one dash) and the teacher can turn it; Reset view returns to the textbook view.
_Avoid_: Camera, rotation, orientation (fine in help text)

**Lone pair lobe**:
A central atom's lone pair drawn as a balloon pointing out from the atom, with two dots in it. The central atom's lone pairs can instead be drawn as two dots or left off; outer atoms' lone pairs are left off unless the teacher turns them on.
_Avoid_: Orbital (that's an orbital diagram's), cloud

**Shape name** / **Electron geometry**:
The name of a VSEPR figure's shape by where its atoms are (trigonal pyramidal for NH₃), and by where all its electron groups are, lone pairs included (tetrahedral for NH₃). Either can be written under the figure, with its **AXE notation** (AX₃E).
_Avoid_: Molecular geometry (fine in search keywords), VSEPR shape

**Bond angle**:
An arc between two bonds with the angle written beside it, one for each kind of angle the shape has (trigonal bipyramidal has a 90° and a 120°). The figure is always drawn at the ideal angles of its electron geometry, lone pairs or not, so only the label says an angle is smaller: it reads the ideal angle, with "<" when lone pairs squeeze it (<109.5° for H₂O), until the teacher types their own (104.5°). A radical's lone electron squeezes nothing, so a shape with one has no "<" (NO₂ reads 120°; it is really 134°), and a period 2 atom with three atoms and a lone electron (CH₃•) is trigonal planar. There's no table of measured angles.
_Avoid_: Angle label, real angle

In a "name the shape" question the shape name, electron geometry, AXE notation and bond angles that are on are left blank, and the answer key fills them in. Outer atoms of different elements go where the usual rule puts them (in a trigonal bipyramid the more electronegative axial and lone pairs equatorial; in a square pyramid the odd atom out at the apex, opposite the lone pair, as O in XeOF₄, however the formula is typed), and the teacher can't swap them. A question needs a label that's on to leave blank; with none, it isn't one. A figure is one molecule or ion.

### Bohr models

**Bohr model**:
A figure of one atom or ion: a nucleus of protons and neutrons with electrons as dots on rings around it. The generator is Bohr Model. The teacher picks an element or sets every count by hand, and nothing is checked, so "wrong" atoms (an overfilled shell, an empty inner shell, 0 protons) draw exactly as set. There is no answer key; the chart title can name the atom. Its nucleus and ring spacing never change, so every Bohr model with the same number of shells is the same size and answer choices made one at a time line up.
_Avoid_: Atom (that's a particle diagram's disc), atomic model (fine in search keywords), atom builder

**Nucleus**:
The center of a Bohr model, drawn either as text (the proton and neutron counts in black in a pale gray circle, never an element symbol) or as a cluster of proton and neutron balls, mixed at random from a seed with a Shuffle button as in a particle diagram. Balls are drawn only up to 40 protons and neutrons combined; above that the nucleus is text and the settings say so. It can be left blank (the empty pale circle) for "how many protons?" questions.
_Avoid_: Core, center

**Proton** / **Neutron** / **Electron**:
The three components of a Bohr model, each with a color from a short palette (red proton, gray neutron and blue electron by default) and an optional symbol: +, p or p⁺ for a proton; 0, n or n⁰ for a neutron; −, e or e⁻ for an electron. An electron with a symbol is drawn as a larger dot so the symbol fits. Protons and neutrons are each 0 to 200.
_Avoid_: Particle (that's a particle diagram's), nucleon (fine in help text)

**Shell**:
One ring of a Bohr model, numbered from the nucleus out, holding 0 to 32 electrons. The teacher sets how many shells there are (1 to 7) and the electrons on each; a shell may be empty even with electrons outside it. Fill sets them to the neutral atom's real ground-state counts for its proton count (K is 2, 8, 8, 1; Fe is 2, 8, 14, 2). Electrons sit evenly spaced around the ring, or paired at the four compass points as in Lewis structures (up to 8; more are spaced evenly). The rings can be drawn empty for "draw the electrons" questions, and can be labeled n = 1, n = 2….
_Avoid_: Orbit, energy level (fine in help text), orbital (a different idea)

**Element**:
Picking one sets a Bohr model's protons to its atomic number, its neutrons to its mass number minus that, and its shells as Fill does. The mass number is the element's atomic mass rounded to a whole number (Cl is 35.45, so 35 and 18 neutrons), or for an element with no stable isotope the bracketed mass number periodic tables show (Tc is [98]). The element isn't stored: it is whichever element has the proton count, so every count can still be changed afterwards.
_Avoid_: Atom (that's a particle diagram's disc), isotope (fine in help text)

**Charge**:
A Bohr model's protons minus its electrons, never stored: fewer electrons than protons is a cation, more is an anion. Setting it (−4 to 8) sets the shells to that ion's ground state, losing and gaining electrons as an orbital diagram's ion does, so Fe²⁺ loses its two 4s electrons (2, 8, 14) and Fe³⁺ one 3d as well (2, 8, 13). A shell the ion empties is left out (Na⁺ is 2, 8), as textbooks draw it.
_Avoid_: Oxidation number, ion charge

**Gained electron** / **Lost electron**:
How a Bohr model's shells differ from its neutral atom's, which the teacher can choose to show. On each shell, electrons beyond the neutral atom's count are gained and drawn in their own color; electrons short of it are lost and drawn as empty dashed spots where they were, on a ring kept for them even when nothing else is left on it (Na⁺ keeps its third ring). The electrons are placed as for whichever of the two has more, so the ones that stay don't move and a gained one takes the next place in filling order (Cl⁻'s completes the last pair). It is worked out from the counts, so a model set by hand shows it too, including one that has lost on one shell and gained on another. It is shown only when the proton count is an element's, and not with empty rings.
_Avoid_: Added electron, removed electron, missing electron, hole

**Brackets**:
Square brackets around an ion's Bohr model with its charge at the top right, as a polyatomic ion's Lewis structure has. A neutral atom is never bracketed.

A Bohr model can have a **key** like a particle diagram's: one proton, neutron and electron drawn exactly as in the figure, with its name, and a gained and a lost electron when the figure shows them.

### Nuclide notation

**Nuclide symbol**:
A figure of one isotope, ion or nuclear particle written as it's typeset: the mass number at the top left, the atomic number at the bottom left, both right-aligned against the symbol, and the charge at the top right (²³⁵₉₂U, ³⁷₁₇Cl⁻). The generator is Nuclide Notation. Only the atomic number, mass number and charge are stored; the protons, neutrons and electrons are worked out from them, and typing one sets them (more protons keep the neutrons and electrons, so the mass number and charge go up). Every nuclide symbol in the same font and size is the same height with its symbol on the same baseline, whatever is left off, so a row of them could line up later for decay equations, which aren't drawn yet.
_Avoid_: Isotope symbol, nuclear notation, AZX notation, complete chemical symbol (all fine in search keywords), element card (a different figure)

**Element** (in nuclide notation):
Any of the 118 elements, picked by atomic number. Picking one sets its mass number to the usual one, as for a Bohr model (Cl is 35), and keeps the charge. The mass number can then be any whole number from the atomic number to 300, so unlikely isotopes draw; one below the atomic number would mean fewer than 0 neutrons, so the page refuses it with a message and the figure doesn't change. For the elements Mass Spectrum has isotopes for, their natural isotopes are buttons (Cl-35, Cl-37), and a mass number that isn't one of them is noted but still drawn.
_Avoid_: Atom

**Your own symbol**:
Up to three characters the teacher types in place of an element's symbol, such as X, Q or ?, with any atomic number from 0, for "identify the element" questions. The answer key names the element it stands for, when there is one ("X is selenium-79 ion, Se²⁻").
_Avoid_: Custom element, unknown, mystery element

**Nuclear particle**:
An alpha particle (⁴₂He or ⁴₂α), beta particle (⁰₋₁e or ⁰₋₁β), positron (⁰₊₁e or ⁰₊₁β), neutron (¹₀n), proton (¹₁p or ¹₁H) or gamma ray (⁰₀γ), each a fixed symbol and pair of numbers as written in decay equations. The bottom number is the particle's charge, so a beta particle's is −1 and a positron's is written +1. A particle has no charge position and no hyphen notation.
_Avoid_: Radiation, decay particle (fine in help text)

**Charge** (in nuclide notation):
The protons minus the electrons, −8 to 8 and never more than the atomic number (fewer than 0 electrons), written as chemists write it: 2+, − or 3−, never +2 or 1+. It's drawn as a raised tspan straight after the symbol, so it sits where the font ends the symbol; a neutral atom shows none.
_Avoid_: Oxidation number, ion charge

**Position**:
One of the four places a nuclide symbol has: the mass number, atomic number, symbol and charge. Each is shown, blank or off (left out, as in ¹⁴C without its atomic number). A **blank** is an empty box by default, or a line to write on, the same size whatever it stands for, so it never hints at the answer. A blank's value is never in the figure or its screen-reader label, only in the answer key.
_Avoid_: Slot, field, missing number

**Hyphen notation**:
An isotope written as its name or symbol and mass number joined by a hyphen, carbon-14 or C-14, on its own or as a smaller line under the symbol. It follows the symbol's and mass number's positions, so a blank mass number is carbon-[blank]; it never shows the atomic number or charge.
_Avoid_: Isotope name, dash notation

The figure is in a **sans-serif** (Arial) or **serif** (Times New Roman) font, in small, medium or large text, which is how large it pastes into a document. The **answer key** gives what it is ("Chlorine-37 ion, Cl⁻"), its protons, neutrons and electrons (a particle's mass number and charge), and each blank's value.

### Dot-and-cross diagrams

**Dot-and-cross diagram**:
A UK-style bonding figure of one covalent molecule, one binary ionic compound, or one atom or ion: each atom's shell drawn as a circle with its element symbol in the middle, and each electron as a mark showing which atom it came from. The generator is Dot-and-Cross Diagram. It draws outer shells only unless the teacher picks all shells; an atom or ion on its own has its own setting and starts with all shells. The teacher types a formula or a name (H2O, ethene, MgCl2, magnesium chloride, Na, Cl-); one element alone is its atom or ion, a formula with a metal is ionic, and anything else is a molecule.
_Avoid_: Lewis dot diagram (that's a Lewis structure's), electron dot diagram, Bohr diagram (that's a Bohr model)

**Mark**:
How one electron is drawn: a dot, a cross, an open circle or an open square, the same for every electron of one atom. In a molecule the central atom's electrons are dots, the outer atoms' crosses, a third kind of atom's open circles (CH₃OH's O, HCN's N) and a fourth's open squares; atoms of one element share a mark unless two of them bond to each other, so Cl₂ is one dots and one crosses and in C₂H₄ the second C has open circles. In an ionic compound the metal's electrons are crosses and the non-metal's dots. An atom on its own has dots, and an anion's gained electrons are crosses. Swap trades dots and crosses. Marks are black unless the teacher gives each kind its own color. An edit can change one electron's mark (see Edit).
_Avoid_: Symbol (that's the element's), electron (fine in help text), dot (alone)

**Overlap**:
Where two bonded atoms' outer circles cross, holding their shared pairs: a row for each pair, the electron from each atom on its own side. Bonded atoms are 1.5 outer radii apart, so a triple bond's three pairs fit, and every circle is the same size, hydrogen's too, as Cambridge mark schemes draw them. Atoms that aren't bonded never overlap: where two H circles would (C₂H₆, ethanol, methylamine, hydrazine), every H circle in that molecule shrinks by the same amount, just enough to clear, and moves in along its bond so its overlap keeps room for its pair, its symbol moving out of the way. A molecule that would still overlap with H circles 0.62 of full size (CH₃COOH) isn't drawn. With all shells the circles grow so the inner rings stay clear of every overlap.
_Avoid_: Intersection, lens

**Shared pair** / **Lone pair**:
Two electrons in an overlap, one from each atom; or two on an atom's own circle, on its free sides as a Lewis structure places them. A double bond is two shared pairs, a triple bond three.
_Avoid_: Bond (fine in help text), nonbonding pair

**Drawn molecule**:
A molecule Lewis Structures builds (octet rule, flat) with no charge, no atom past an octet and every formal charge 0, so each atom gives one electron to each shared pair and keeps its lone electrons. Molecules that need a dative bond (CO, SO₂, O₃), polyatomic ions (NH₄⁺) and expanded octets (SF₆) get a message and the Request it button.
_Avoid_: Supported molecule

**Ionic compound**:
A metal (Li, Na, K, Rb, Mg, Ca, Sr or Al) with a non-metal (N, O, F, S, Cl, Br or I), written either way round. Each ion's charge comes from its group (Na⁺, Mg²⁺, Al³⁺, N³⁻, O²⁻, Cl⁻) and the charges must balance: MgCl is refused, and the page says MgCl₂ balances. A metal ion shows the full shell it is left with (Na⁺ is a ring of 8 crosses); a non-metal ion has a full outer shell, its gained electrons taking the last places to fill, turned to face a metal ion beside it. Compounds with polyatomic ions (NaOH) aren't drawn.
_Avoid_: Salt, ionic molecule, formula unit

**Ion layout**:
How an ionic compound's ions are arranged: every ion, the single ion in the middle (Cl⁻ Mg²⁺ Cl⁻, Na⁺ O²⁻ Na⁺), the third of three around one below the middle, and 2:3 compounds alternating; or numbered, one of each ion with its number in front, as 2[Al]³⁺ 3[O]²⁻. Compounds of up to three ions start with every ion, bigger ones numbered, and the teacher can pick either.
_Avoid_: Layout (alone; that's a particle diagram's), arrangement (that's an orbital diagram's)

**Atom or ion** (dot-and-cross):
One element from H to Ca, where shells fill 2, 8, 8, with its electrons on its rings, paired at the top, right, bottom and left as a Bohr model's paired placement puts them; or the element's usual ion from its group, in brackets with its charge.
_Avoid_: Bohr model (that has a nucleus, not a symbol)

**Brackets** (dot-and-cross):
Square brackets around every ion with its charge at the top right. The charge can be written, left blank (an empty box for students to write it in) or left off, for every ion or, by an edit, for one; the brackets stay.

**Blank** (dot-and-cross):
The circles, overlaps, symbols and brackets with no electrons, for students to draw them. The figure's description for screen readers leaves the electrons out too.
_Avoid_: Empty rings (that's a Bohr model's), scaffold

**Answer key** (dot-and-cross):
The lines under the figure: each kind of atom's bonding and lone pairs for a molecule ("O: 2 bonding pairs, 2 lone pairs"); who loses and gains how many electrons and each ion's arrangement for an ionic compound ("Mg loses 2 electrons, one to each Cl." and "Mg²⁺ 2,8; Cl⁻ 2,8,8"); the arrangement for an atom or ion. It always describes the correct diagram; when an edit changes what's drawn it ends "This figure was edited by hand, so it may differ from the correct diagram described here."

**Edit** (dot-and-cross):
A change the teacher makes by clicking the figure, usually for a "find the mistake" question. Clicking an electron cycles what's drawn: dot, cross, open circle, open square, none (not drawn), then a dot again. Clicking an element symbol leaves it blank or shows it again; clicking an ion's charge cycles shown, blank box, left off. An electron, symbol or charge edited away keeps its click target, so it can be clicked back. Swap still trades an edited dot or cross, and with color an edited mark takes the color of the atom whose mark that is in this figure, or black if no atom has it. With the electrons blank they can't be clicked, though their edits are kept. Edits go in the address as one setting, each by the item's place in the figure's lists of electrons, symbols and charges; those places change with the formula, the molecule picked, the layout and the shells, so changing any of them clears the edits, and places the figure doesn't have are ignored. The description for screen readers says what's drawn after edits, counting the marks as drawn and naming no blank symbol (nor the molecule then). Reset edits clears them all.
_Avoid_: Change (that's an orbital diagram's), mistake (the teacher may edit for other reasons), override

### Orbital diagrams

**Orbital diagram**:
A figure of one atom or ion's electrons: a square (or line) for each orbital, the orbitals of a sublevel side by side and labeled under them, with each electron drawn as an up or down arrow. The generator is Orbital Diagram. Bohr models, energy-level diagrams and Lewis dot diagrams aren't drawn.
_Avoid_: Electron diagram, box diagram, orbital notation (both fine in search keywords), orbital box diagram

**Sublevel**:
The orbitals sharing a number and letter, such as 2p: s has 1 orbital, p has 3, d has 5, f has 7.
_Avoid_: Subshell (fine in search keywords), orbital (that's one of them)

**Orbital**:
One square (or line) in an orbital diagram, holding up to two electrons.
_Avoid_: Box (that's a particle diagram's container), square

**Electron**:
One arrow in an orbital, pointing up or down for its spin. A lone electron is drawn in the middle of its orbital; a pair is drawn up then down.
_Avoid_: Arrow (fine in help text), dot

**Configuration**:
How many electrons each sublevel holds, written as 1s² 2s² 2p⁴. The ground-state configuration comes from the element, the charge and the configuration rule.
_Avoid_: Electron arrangement, notation

**Filling order**:
The order sublevels fill in by the aufbau principle: 1s 2s 2p 3s 3p 4s 3d 4p 5s 4d 5p 6s 4f 5d 6p 7s 5f 6d 7p. The diagram draws sublevels in this order unless the teacher picks shell order (3d before 4s).
_Avoid_: Aufbau order (fine in help text), energy order, Madelung order

**Arrangement**:
How the sublevels are laid out: in a row, side by side and wrapping onto more rows (the default), or by energy, each sublevel a step higher than the one before it in filling order with s, p, d and f in their own columns, and the noble gas core at the bottom. Stacked by energy, the sublevel order only changes the written configuration.
_Avoid_: Energy diagram, energy-level diagram, layout

**Exception**:
An element whose real ground state doesn't follow the filling order, such as Cr ([Ar] 4s¹ 3d⁵) or Cu ([Ar] 4s¹ 3d¹⁰).
_Avoid_: Anomaly, irregular element

**Configuration rule**:
Whether the generator draws exceptions as they really are (the default) or as the filling order predicts, for classes that don't teach exceptions. The check follows the same rule.
_Avoid_: Exception mode

**Noble gas core**:
The electrons of the largest noble gas with fewer electrons than the atom or ion, written in brackets ([Ar]) instead of drawn. Off by default.
_Avoid_: Shorthand, abbreviated configuration, kernel

**Scaffold**:
What an orbital diagram figure leaves for the student: the symbol, the sublevel labels or the written configuration as a blank line, or the orbitals drawn empty.
_Avoid_: Blank, hint

**Change**:
A teacher's edit to one orbital's electrons: empty, up, down, a pair, or two with the same spin. A diagram with any changes is a **changed diagram**.
_Avoid_: Edit, error

**Mistake**:
One way a changed diagram differs from the ground state, written as a sentence naming the rule ("Hund's rule: 2p has a pair while one of its orbitals is empty"). A changed diagram with the right number of electrons and no Pauli exclusion mistake is an **excited state**; one breaking Pauli exclusion is **not allowed**.
_Avoid_: Error, violation (fine in help text)

### Titration curves

**Titration curve**:
A graph of the flask's pH (up the side) against the volume of titrant added in mL (along the bottom), for one monoprotic acid or base titrated with a strong base or acid. The grid, axes and titles are the same as Math's coordinate grid, from `$shared/graph`.
_Avoid_: pH curve, titration graph (fine in search keywords)

**Analyte**:
What's in the flask: a strong or weak acid, or a strong or weak base. An acid is titrated with NaOH and its pH rises; a base is titrated with HCl and its pH falls.
_Avoid_: Sample, unknown

**Titrant**:
The strong base or acid added from the buret.
_Avoid_: Reagent

**Concentrations**:
The first way to give a curve: the analyte's molarity and volume, the titrant's molarity, and pKa for a weak acid or pKb for a weak base. The pH at each volume is solved exactly from the charge balance, so a weak acid's half-equivalence point lands on its pKa. A **common titration** (HCl, CH₃COOH, HCOOH or HF with NaOH, or NaOH or NH₃ with HCl, 25.0 mL of 0.100 M with 0.100 M) fills these in.
_Avoid_: Chemistry mode, calculated

**Key points**:
The second way to give a curve: the starting pH, the equivalence point's volume and pH, and the ending pH, which is the pH at the end of the x-axis. The curve is the real titration whose own curve comes closest to these, stretched on each side of the equivalence point the rest of the way through each. Going back to concentrations fills in that titration's (to 3 significant figures), unless the key points still come from the concentrations already there; a note says which key points its curve misses, and why when it can.
_Avoid_: Manual mode, custom points

**Equivalence point**:
Where the titrant added has exactly neutralized the analyte: the middle of the steep part of the curve.
_Avoid_: End point (that's where an indicator changes color)

**Half-equivalence point**:
Halfway to the equivalence point, where a weak acid's pH equals its pKa (for a weak base, 14 − pKb). Marked only for weak acids and bases.
_Avoid_: Midpoint, buffer point

**Marked point**:
The equivalence or half-equivalence point drawn as a dot, alone or with dashed lines to both axes, with a label, a blank line for students, or nothing.
_Avoid_: Marker, annotation

### Line spectra

**Line spectrum**:
A figure of one or more strips stacked on one wavelength axis, each showing the lines of the light an element gives off or takes in, so students can match an unknown's lines to known elements. The generator is Line Spectrum.
_Avoid_: Bright-line spectrum (that's the emission style; fine in search keywords), spectrograph, spectral chart

**Strip**:
One horizontal band of a line spectrum with its label to its left: an element strip, a custom strip or a mixture strip. Every strip is the same width whatever the range, and a wavelength sits at the same place on every strip, so lines can be matched straight down. A figure has one to eight.
_Avoid_: Row, band, bar, spectrum (alone)

**Line**:
One thin vertical mark on a strip at one wavelength, in nm to 0.1. Lines outside the axis's range aren't drawn, and the page says how many are hidden.
_Avoid_: Peak, band, spectral line (fine in help text)

**Element strip**:
A strip showing a few of one element's strongest visible lines (3 to 8), not every line it has, taken from the NIST Atomic Spectra Database for the neutral atom (see `elements.ts`): H, He, Li, Na, K, Ca, Sr, Ba, Cu, Zn, Cd, Hg, Ne, Ar and Kr. Flame test colors can differ from these lines, since some come from molecules in the flame. Editing its lines makes it a custom strip with the same lines.
_Avoid_: Preset (that's a teacher's saved settings), reference spectrum

**Custom strip**:
A strip of wavelengths the teacher types, with a name of their own such as "Element X", for elements students can't look up or for trimmed-down spectra. Anything typed that isn't a wavelength from 100 to 2000 nm is left out and named on the page.
_Avoid_: Own lines (fine as the button), made-up element

**Mixture strip**:
A strip showing every line of the element and custom strips the teacher ticks, named "Unknown" unless the teacher renames it, for students to work out what's in it. Where two parts share a wavelength, it is one line. A mixture can't include another mixture.
_Avoid_: Unknown (that's its default name), combined spectrum, sample

**Style**:
How strips are drawn: emission (colored lines on black, as a glowing gas gives off), absorption (black lines across a rainbow, as a cool gas takes out of white light) or print (black lines on white, for a black-and-white copier). Each wavelength's color is Dan Bruton's approximation, dimming toward 380 and 780 nm.
_Avoid_: Mode, theme, bright-line/dark-line (fine in help text)

**Brightness**:
All lines equally bright, the default, so only where they are matters; or relative, each element line as bright as NIST's intensity for it against the element's strongest line, never under 30%. NIST's intensities are rough and come from a different source for each element, so they compare lines of one element only. Custom lines are always full strength.
_Avoid_: Intensity (fine in help text), opacity

**Strip labels**:
What's written left of each element strip: its name, its symbol, a blank line for students, or nothing. Custom and mixture strips show the names the teacher gave them, except when labels are off altogether.
_Avoid_: Legend, key, caption

**Wavelength axis**:
The scale in nm under the last strip, under every strip, or left off, from 400 to 700 nm unless the teacher changes it (within 380 to 780, at least 20 nm wide), with ticks and numbers at multiples of their spacing and the title "Wavelength (nm)". Shortest wavelength (violet) is on the left.
_Avoid_: x-axis, ruler

**Answer key** (line spectra):
The lines under the figure naming what's in each mixture ("Unknown: hydrogen and sodium") and, when element strips' labels are blank, the elements from the top down. A mixture's parts are never in the figure's description for screen readers.

### Photoelectron spectra

**Photoelectron spectrum**:
A graph of how many electrons an atom gives up at each binding energy, as in AP Chemistry: one peak for each sublevel of the element's ground state, as tall as that sublevel's electrons, binding energy along the bottom falling from left to right. The generator is Photoelectron Spectrum, for neutral atoms from H to Xe.
_Avoid_: PES (fine in search keywords and help text), photoelectron graph

**Peak**:
One sublevel's electrons on a photoelectron spectrum, drawn as a narrow smooth peak or a bar. Its height is the sublevel's number of electrons; where it sits is the sublevel's binding energy. Its label is the sublevel (2p), with its electrons (2p⁶) or its energy over it if the teacher wants, or a blank line for students.
_Avoid_: Spike, line, band

**Binding energy**:
The energy it takes to remove one electron from a sublevel of the free atom, in MJ/mol (the default, as AP writes it) or eV (1 MJ/mol is 10.36 eV). H to Ca are the textbook table AP materials quote (Ne: 84.0, 4.68, 2.08); Sc to Xe are Lotz (1970), with a p or d sublevel's two values averaged into one peak (ADR 0006).
_Avoid_: Ionization energy (that's the lowest one only; fine in help text), energy level

**Energy axis**:
The binding energy scale along the bottom: logarithmic (each step ten times the last, the default), broken into a linear stretch for each group of peaks with break marks between, or linear from 0. Core and valence energies differ a thousandfold, so only the first two show every peak apart (ADR 0006).
_Avoid_: x-axis (fine in help text), scale (that's how it's spaced)

**Compared element**:
A second element drawn dashed and gray behind the first, for "why are magnesium's peaks to the left of sodium's?" questions. The key names both, or calls them Element A and Element B when names are hidden.
_Avoid_: Overlay, second spectrum

### Heating and cooling curves

**Heating curve** / **Cooling curve**:
A graph of a substance's temperature in °C (up the side) as it is heated or cooled steadily, against the time or the heat added or removed (along the bottom). Each phase warms or cools in a sloped segment; each phase change is a flat plateau. A cooling curve is the same segments run from the hot end. The generator is Heating and Cooling Curve. The grid, axes and titles are the same as a titration curve's, from `$shared/graph`, except that the axes always run along the grid's left and bottom edges, so temperatures below 0 °C don't move the time axis up into the graph.
_Avoid_: Phase change graph, temperature graph (both fine in search keywords), phase diagram (a different figure)

**Segment**:
One piece of a curve: a phase warming or cooling (solid, liquid, gas), or a plateau (melting or boiling heating, freezing or condensing cooling). A curve has only the segments its starting and ending temperatures pass through, and includes a plateau it starts or ends right at, so ice at 0 °C begins by melting.
_Avoid_: Section, part, step

**Plateau**:
A flat segment at the melting (freezing) or boiling point, where both phases are present and the temperature doesn't change.
_Avoid_: Flat part, phase change line

**Substance**:
What's heated or cooled: one of the substance setups, or Custom, the teacher's own melting and boiling points (and, for a curve to scale, its properties). A **substance setup** is water, ethanol, acetone, mercury, sodium chloride or Substance X (made up, with round numbers: 40 and 120 °C), each with its melting and boiling points and properties, cited in `curve.ts`. Picking one runs the curve from below its melting point to above its boiling point and fits the axes to it; picking Custom copies the setup's values into the fields, so the curve doesn't change until the teacher types.
_Avoid_: Preset (that's a teacher's saved settings), material, compound

**Schematic**:
The first way to lay out the segments, and the default: how long each runs along the x-axis, typed, for the simple worksheet curve drawn to no particular scale (2, 3, 5, 8 and 2 to start, the boiling plateau longest as in textbooks). Switching to it from to scale copies that curve's lengths (to 3 significant figures).
_Avoid_: Manual mode, segment lengths (fine in help text)

**To scale**:
The second way: each segment takes q = m·c·ΔT or q = n·ΔH from the sample's mass and the substance's specific heats of each phase, enthalpies of fusion and vaporization and molar mass, so the curve is to scale. With time along the x-axis, a steady rate of heat in kJ per minute turns heat into time. A specific heat is the phase's near the temperatures its segment covers where that's known, otherwise at 25 °C, so the curve is close but not exact. Water's values are OpenStax Chemistry 2e's: 2.09, 4.18 and 1.86 J/(g·°C), 6.01 and 40.67 kJ/mol, 18.02 g/mol. The heat each segment takes is listed under the fields, for an answer key.
_Avoid_: Properties mode, calculated, physical

**Supercooling**:
On a cooling curve that freezes, the liquid cooling past its freezing point by the degrees the teacher types before it starts to freeze, then warming back up to the freezing point. The dip takes the start of the freezing plateau, so the rest of the curve doesn't move.
_Avoid_: Undercooling

**Corner letter**:
A, B, C… at the start of the curve and the end of each segment, for questions like "What is happening between B and C?". It goes outside the turn the curve makes there, or the nearest clear spot.
_Avoid_: Point label, marker

**Segment label**:
A segment's state written beside it (Solid, Liquid, Gas; a plateau as the change, Melting, Boiling, Freezing or Condensing, unless the teacher picks both states, Solid + liquid or Liquid + gas), or a blank line for students. Each goes in the first spot near its segment that's clear of the curve, the dashed lines and the other labels.
_Avoid_: Annotation, caption

**Point label**:
What's written by the temperature axis just above (or below) a plateau's dashed line: its temperature (78.3 °C), m.p. or b.p. (f.p. and b.p. on a cooling curve), or a blank line for students to write the melting or boiling point on. It goes along the line, inside the grid, as far from the axis as the curve makes it. The temperature by default.
_Avoid_: Axis label (that's the letter at an axis's end), tick label

### Phase diagrams

**Phase diagram**:
A graph of one substance's pressure (up the side) against its temperature (along the bottom), split by its phase boundaries into regions. The generator is Phase Diagram. The substance is water, carbon dioxide, nitrogen, oxygen, argon, methane or ammonia, with values from each one's reference equation of state as the NIST Chemistry WebBook gives them (cited in `phase.ts`), or Your own, from six typed values: the triple point, the critical point and the normal melting and boiling points.
_Avoid_: Phase change diagram (a search word only; it is also what teachers call a heating curve), P–T diagram

**Phase boundary**:
One of the three lines between regions: the **sublimation curve**, from the graph's bottom left corner up to the triple point; the **fusion line**, straight from the triple point to the top of the graph, leaning left only for water among the real substances (and for Your own when its melting point is below its triple point's temperature); and the **vaporization curve**, from the triple point to the critical point, rising ever more steeply, where it stops. The sublimation curve arrives at the triple point steeper than the vaporization curve leaves it. Each can be named along it with its changes (Melting / freezing, Boiling / condensing, Sublimation / deposition), lettered beside a small dot on it, or given a blank line.
_Avoid_: Equilibrium line, phase line, coexistence curve

**Region**:
The area of one phase: Solid left of the sublimation curve and fusion line, Liquid between the fusion line and the vaporization curve, Gas below and right. With the supercritical fluid marked, dashed lines run up and right from the critical point and the region past them is Supercritical fluid.
_Avoid_: Vapor (for the Gas region), phase area, zone

**Triple point**:
Where the three phase boundaries meet: the one temperature and pressure where solid, liquid and gas can be present together. A dot, named, lettered, blank or bare, with dashed lines to both axes. A blank line for it is joined to its dot by a short line, so it isn't taken for a region's.

**Critical point**:
Where the vaporization curve ends: past it the liquid and gas can't be told apart. A dot, labeled as the triple point is.

**1 atm line**:
A dashed line across the graph at 1 atm (101.3 kPa), named at the pressure axis (on a grid, whose axis is numbered, on the graph beside the line), with a dot and a dashed line down where it crosses each boundary. Not to scale, a crossing with no value to write at its foot (a real substance's melting point, below) has no line down. For carbon dioxide, whose triple point is above 1 atm, it crosses only the sublimation curve. In the panel it's the "1 atm line".
_Avoid_: Isobar (the setting's name in the address, not in the panel), normal pressure line

**Normal melting point** / **Normal boiling point**:
Where the 1 atm line crosses the fusion line and the vaporization curve: the temperatures a substance melts and boils at under 1 atm. Marked by its temperature, its name (Normal m.p., Normal b.p.), a letter or a blank line. Every real substance but water melts within a few hundredths of a kelvin of its triple point's temperature, so its melting point isn't written apart from the triple point's, and with the temperatures shown its crossing has a dot but no line down to the axis; for Your own both are typed.
_Avoid_: Melting point / boiling point alone (fine in help text), m.p. and b.p. alone

**Not to scale** / **On a grid**:
The two kinds of axes. Not to scale (the default, and the only one for a real substance) is the textbook figure on a fixed block grid with no numbers or gridlines: the triple point low and left, the critical point high and right, 1 atm between, and the real values written at the axes where the dashed lines land. On a grid is Your own substance on numbered axes and gridlines, to scale, its vaporization curve bent to pass through the typed boiling point. Named in the address as Potential Energy Diagram's are, shape and numbered.
_Avoid_: Schematic (that's a heating curve's), log scale, qualitative

### Half-life graphs

**Half-life graph**:
A graph of how much of a radioactive isotope is left (up the side) against time (along the bottom), falling as N = N₀ · (½)^(t / T½): half is left after one half-life, a quarter after two, and it never reaches zero. One curve serves for the share left, the mass, the number of nuclei and the activity, since all fall alike. The generator is Half-Life Graph, also listed on Physics Figures. The grid, axes and titles are the same as a heating curve's, from `$shared/graph`, the axes always along the grid's left and bottom edges; nothing is drawn before time 0.
_Avoid_: Decay curve, decay graph, radioactive decay graph (all fine in search keywords), exponential graph

**Half-life**:
How long it takes half of an isotope's nuclei to decay, written T½. The decay constant, λ = ln 2 / T½, is given in the answer key, not on the figure.
_Avoid_: Half life (unhyphenated), halving time

**Isotope**:
One of up to three radioactive isotopes on a graph, each drawn from time 0 in its own line: solid, dashed or dotted, so three read apart in black and white, with a key under the graph naming them when there's more than one line. Each is an **isotope setup** or Custom, a name, half-life and unit the teacher types. The setups are carbon-14, hydrogen-3, fluorine-18, phosphorus-32, cobalt-60, strontium-90, technetium-99m, iodine-131, cesium-137, radon-222, radium-226, polonium-210, uranium-238, potassium-40, plutonium-239, americium-241 and Isotope X (made up: 10 years, decaying to Isotope Y), with half-lives from NNDC NuDat 3 rounded to three significant figures, cited in `isotopes.ts`, except carbon-14's: the textbook 5,730 years, where NuDat 3 gives 5,700 ± 30. Picking a setup for the first isotope sets the time axis to its unit; any change of isotope fits the time axis. Picking Custom copies the isotope already there, so the curve doesn't change until the teacher types.
_Avoid_: Element, nuclide (fine in help text), sample, preset (that's a teacher's saved settings)

**Hidden names**:
The isotopes called Isotope A, B and C in the key (one alone with its daughter, Parent isotope and Daughter isotope), for students to identify from the half-lives they read off. The real names are in the answer key only, never on the figure or in its description for screen readers.
_Avoid_: Mystery isotope, unknown

**Quantity**:
What's up the side: percent remaining (from 100, the default), fraction remaining (from 1), or the mass in g, the number of nuclei, or the activity in Bq or counts per minute, each from a starting amount the teacher types. The y-axis title follows it ("Percent remaining (%)", "Mass remaining (g)", "Count rate (counts/min)") unless the teacher types their own, as Mass Spectrum's does; the x-axis title follows the time unit ("Time (years)"). Picking a quantity fits the y-axis to it.
_Avoid_: Y-axis (fine in help text), measure, amount (alone)

**Time unit**:
The unit along the x-axis: seconds, minutes, hours, days, years, thousand years or billion years. Each isotope's half-life is turned into it, a year being 365.25 days.
_Avoid_: Scale, x-axis unit

**Fitted axes**:
The axes the Fit button gives: time from 0 to at least five half-lives of the longest-lived isotope drawn, in up to 16 blocks, and the quantity from 0 to the starting amount in up to 10, both counting in round steps (1, 2, 2.5 or 5 times a power of ten). The steps are round, not the half-life, so students read the half-life off the curve; the guide lines show it when the teacher wants it shown.
_Avoid_: Auto axes, zoom

**Guide line**:
A dashed line at one of the first isotope's first one to four half-lives: from 50%, 25%, 12.5% or 6.25% of the starting amount across from the y-axis to the curve, then down to the time axis. Each is labeled by its foot on the time axis, or beside its corner where the curve is in the way, with its time ("5,730 years"), T½, 2T½…, a blank line for students, or nothing. A guide line off the grid is left out, and the page says so. None by default.
_Avoid_: Marked point, dashed line (fine in help text), drop line

**Half-life dot**:
A dot at each of a curve's first six half-lives, in its own marker (circle, square or triangle), and at the start when it's the only curve (several isotopes share the start, and a daughter's is on the axis). A curve whose half-lives would sit almost on top of each other on the time axis gets none. With the curves turned off, the dots alone (or an empty grid) are left for students to draw the curve.
_Avoid_: Data point, marker

**Daughter curve**:
The first isotope's stable daughter, rising as N₀ − N, dashed and named in the key, so it crosses the parent at one half-life. Offered only for one isotope, as a percent, fraction or number of nuclei (a stable daughter has no activity, and an alpha emitter's daughter is lighter than its parent), and only where the daughter is stable or lives far longer than the parent: strontium-90, radon-222, radium-226 and potassium-40 have none. Uranium-238 is drawn going straight to lead-206, its chain's steps all far shorter than its own half-life. A custom isotope's daughter takes the name the teacher types. The y-axis title then says atoms or nuclei, not "remaining".
_Avoid_: Product, decay product (fine in help text), daughter isotope (that's the hidden name)

**Answer key** (half-life graphs):
The lines in the settings panel and on example pages giving each isotope's half-life, decay constant and how it decays and to what, naming each hidden one ("Isotope A is carbon-14"), then what's left of the first isotope at each guide line ("After 3 half-lives (17,190 years): 12.5%").

### Rate law graphs

**Rate law graph**:
A graph against time of one reactant in a reaction A → products with rate = k[A]ⁿ: its concentration [A], ln[A] or 1/[A]. The generator is Rate Law Graphs, which draws the three side by side on the same time axis (each 6 squares tall), or one alone, for students to find the order from which is a straight line. The figure never says the order; the answer key does. The grids are `$shared/graph`'s, the axes along the left and bottom edges (ln[A] is negative below 1 M), always fitted: time from 0 to when a tenth of [A]₀ is left, rounded up to a round step, and each y-axis to its own values. Not the curves of product made or gas collected under different conditions (temperature, catalyst, surface area): those would be a rate of reaction graph, not built.
_Avoid_: Kinetics graph, linearised plot (both fine in search keywords), rate graph (the conditions curves), decay curve (that's Half-Life Graph)

**Order**:
Zero, first or second: the power n of [A] in the rate law. Its integrated rate law makes one graph straight: [A] = [A]₀ − kt for zero order, ln[A] = ln[A]₀ − kt for first, 1/[A] = 1/[A]₀ + kt for second. A zero-order reaction ends when [A] reaches 0, at [A]₀/k; nothing is drawn after it. Third order and pseudo-order aren't drawn.
_Avoid_: Reaction order (fine in help text and search keywords), zeroth order (fine in search keywords), rate order

**Rate constant**:
k, typed by the teacher, in the unit its order gives it, written from the concentration and time units: M s⁻¹, s⁻¹ or M⁻¹ s⁻¹ (mol L⁻¹ min⁻¹, dm³ mol⁻¹ s⁻¹ and so on). Changing the order sets k so the first half-life stays the same (to three significant figures); the time axis then changes, as a tenth of [A]₀ is left after 1.8, 3.3 or 9 half-lives. Where the k it needs is outside 10⁻⁶ to 10⁶, k stops at that end and the page says the half-life couldn't be kept. Where the time axis's numbers run to long strings of zeros (k near either end), the page suggests another time unit or k. The half-lives follow from it: [A]₀/2k, each half as long as the last, for zero order; ln 2/k, all the same, for first; 1/(k[A]₀), each twice the last, for second.
_Avoid_: Rate coefficient, k value

**Straight graph**:
The one of the three graphs that is a straight line for the order: [A] for zero order, ln[A] for first, 1/[A] for second, with slope −k, −k and +k. The answer key names it with its slope and intercept.
_Avoid_: Linear plot, linearised graph, best fit

**Data points**:
5 to 10 measurements of [A], evenly spaced from time 0 at a plain spacing (every 12 s, not every 14.3 s) to the end of the time axis, or for zero order to when a tenth of [A]₀ is left (where its ln[A] and 1/[A] axes are fitted to, so no point runs off them or reads [A] = 0), drawn as dots on each graph, in a data table under the graphs (time and [A], or ln[A] and 1/[A] too), or both. Each [A] is the model's value with any scatter, rounded to three significant figures, and ln[A] and 1/[A] are worked out from that rounded [A], as a student would: ln[A] to three decimal places (a log has as many decimal places as its number has significant figures), 1/[A] to three significant figures; the first is [A]₀ exactly. With a table, the screen reader's description reads the table out. With the curve off they are the data for students to judge; with the dots off too and the table on, the grids are empty for students to plot. The answer key gives r² of each graph's points.
_Avoid_: Half-life dots (that's Half-Life Graph), measurements (fine in help text), readings

**Scatter**:
About 1, 2 or 5% random error on each data point's [A], drawn from a seed kept in the address (`docs/adr/0002-particles-placed-from-settings.md`), so a link redraws the same data; New data draws another seed. When the scatter makes another graph's points straighter than the right one's, the page says so.
_Avoid_: Noise (that's the address parameter's name only), error, random data

**Guide line** (rate law graphs):
As on a half-life graph: a dashed line at one of the first one to three half-lives, from [A]₀/2, [A]₀/4 or [A]₀/8 across to the curve and down to the time axis, on the [A] graph only, labeled with its time, how many half-lives, a blank line or nothing. Where the labels can't all fit (three on a small graph in the row), the page says so.

**Answer key** (rate law graphs):
The lines in the settings panel and on example pages: the order and rate law ("First order: rate = k[A]"), the integrated rate law, k with its unit, the straight graph with its slope and intercept, the half-life, [A] at each guide line, and with data, r² of each graph's points and the scatter.

### Equilibrium graphs

**Equilibrium graph**:
A graph of every species' concentration in mol/L (up the side) against time (along the bottom) for a reversible reaction: settling to equilibrium from its starting concentrations, then answering up to three **stresses**. The generator is Equilibrium Graph. The graphs are solved from K, never drawn from typed levels: every level stretch gives the K it should and the lines always move in the ratio of the coefficients (`model.ts`). Each species is its own line, solid, dashed or dotted in the order of the equation, in color or all black, with its formula beside it where it first levels off unless the teacher turns the formulas off. The grid and titles are `$shared/graph`'s, as a half-life graph's.
_Avoid_: Le Châtelier graph, concentration–time graph (both fine in search keywords), plateau (for an equilibrium; fine in help text)

**Reaction setup**:
One of the seven reactions a graph can show: N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃, H₂ + I₂ ⇌ 2HI, N₂O₄ ⇌ 2NO₂, 2SO₂ + O₂ ⇌ 2SO₃, PCl₅ ⇌ PCl₃ + Cl₂, Fe³⁺ + SCN⁻ ⇌ FeSCN²⁺ (the one in solution) and the made-up A + B ⇌ C, which stands in for any other. Each has its species, coefficients, its real direction of heat (cited in `reactions.ts`, and shown under the K box with what backs it up: ΔH°, such as "exothermic: ΔH° = −92 kJ/mol", or for the iron(III) thiocyanate demonstration "the red color fades on warming"; A + B ⇌ C's is the teacher's to choose) and an **example K** with example starting concentrations, which picking it fills in. The teacher can't type a reaction of their own.
_Avoid_: Preset (that's a teacher's saved settings), equation (fine in help text), system

**Example K**:
The K a reaction setup starts with, chosen with its starting concentrations so the lines level off well apart: not the reaction's measured equilibrium constant. The page says so in grey text always shown under the K box, in the answer key's first line ("K = 0.5 (an example value, not measured)"), in the About copy and in `/linking`; it is never written on the figure. The note stays when the teacher types their own K. K is Kc, from concentrations.
_Avoid_: Real K, default K, Keq (fine in search keywords)

**Stress**:
What's done to the system at a time the teacher types: a species added or removed (that line alone jumps; a removal never goes below 0), the volume changed by a factor (every concentration jumps by 1 over it at once; for the solution setup it's water added or evaporated), or the temperature raised or lowered (no jump: K is multiplied or divided by a factor, down on heating an exothermic reaction, and the approach runs faster when heated, slower when cooled). Each is marked with a dashed vertical line with its words above the grid: the page's own ("N₂ added", "Volume halved", "Temperature raised") until the teacher types theirs, or none, for students to say what happened. Two stresses at the same moment act together: the system shifts once, answering both.
_Avoid_: Disturbance, perturbation (both search words only), pressure change (it's a volume change here), change (alone)

**Shift**:
Which way the reaction runs after a stress to reach its new equilibrium: right, toward the products; left, toward the reactants; or no shift (a volume change with the same moles of gas on both sides, H₂ + I₂ ⇌ 2HI). The answer key gives it for each stress, with the new equilibrium concentrations, and the new K after a temperature change.
_Avoid_: Response, direction (alone)

**Established**:
The time equilibrium is first reached, the first approach coming within 1% of it, typed by the teacher (4 by default). It sets how fast the whole graph runs: the same rate constants carry every later shift, so a shift can take longer or shorter than the first approach. The page says when a stress comes before the system has settled, or the time axis ends mid-shift.
_Avoid_: Equilibrium time, rate (alone)

**Extent** (code only):
The one number that moves between stresses, ξ: each concentration is its start plus its coefficient × ξ, reactants minus. Where it settles is the ξ where Q = K; how it gets there is the reaction taken as elementary, rate forward minus rate reverse. Not a word on the page.
_Avoid_: Progress, x (fine in an ICE table)

**Fitted axes** (equilibrium graphs):
What picking a setup, adding or removing a stress, and the Fit button give: time from 0 until the last shift has settled and stayed level as long again, concentration from 0 to a little over the highest any line goes, both in round steps. Typing a number never moves the axes.
_Avoid_: Auto-scale, zoom

**Answer key** (equilibrium graphs):
The lines in the settings panel and on example pages: K, marked an example value, and the equilibrium concentrations first reached; then for each stress what it did, which way the system shifts, the new K after a temperature change and the new equilibrium concentrations, to three significant figures. A removal of more than is there says all of it went, and how much that was; the first of two stresses at the same moment shares the second's shift.

### Potential energy diagrams

**Potential energy diagram**:
A graph of the potential energy of the reacting particles (up the side, in kJ/mol, kJ mol⁻¹ or kJ) against the reaction coordinate (along the bottom) as a reaction goes from reactants to products: a smooth curve, flat at the reactants, over each transition state and down to the products. The generator is Potential Energy Diagram, also listed on Biology for activation energy with and without an enzyme. The grid and titles are `$shared/graph`'s, with the axes along the grid's edges as on a heating curve.
_Avoid_: Reaction energy profile, energy profile, enthalpy profile, reaction coordinate diagram (all fine in search keywords and help text), energy graph, bond energy curve (a different figure)

**Reaction coordinate**:
The x-axis: how far the reaction has gone, from reactants to products. It isn't a time or any quantity with units, so it's never numbered and has no gridlines; each step takes the same width, with flat runs at both ends.
_Avoid_: Time, progress axis

**Level**:
One energy the curve passes through, typed by the teacher: the reactants, a transition state, an intermediate or the products, in order along the reaction. Only the levels are kept (in the address as `levels=50_150_20`); Ea and ΔH are worked out from them, and typing Ea or ΔH moves the transition state or the products to match. The curve between neighboring levels is a smoothstep, so each peak and dip is exactly at its level and nothing overshoots.
_Avoid_: Point, value

**Transition state**:
The peak of a step, its highest energy. A transition state must be above the levels either side of it, or the page says so. The usual word, which the teacher can change to "Activated complex".
_Avoid_: Peak (fine in help text), activated complex (fine as a label)

**Intermediate**:
The dip between two steps of a mechanism, below the transition states either side of it.
_Avoid_: Trough

**Step**:
One rise to a transition state and fall from it. A diagram has one, two or three. Each step's Ea runs from the level before it up to its transition state; its reverse Ea from the level after it. The answer key names the **rate-determining step** only when the step with the highest Ea is also the one whose transition state is highest above the lowest point before it.
_Avoid_: Stage, hump

**Catalysed path**:
A second curve, dashed, with the same reactants and products and one lower transition state, so ΔH doesn't change and both activation energies drop by the same amount. Its transition state must be above the reactants and products and below the highest uncatalysed one. A key under the graph names the two paths ("Uncatalysed" and "Catalysed", or the teacher's words, such as "Without enzyme" and "With enzyme").
_Avoid_: Catalyst curve, second curve

**Arrow**:
A vertical arrow marking an energy difference: each step's Ea, the catalysed Ea, ΔH (from the reactants to the products, pointing toward the products) and the reverse Ea (of the last step). Each is labeled with its symbol (Eₐ, ΔH), its value ("ΔH = −30 kJ/mol"), a letter or number for "which arrow shows…?" questions, a blank line, or nothing, beside it where there's room, otherwise in the nearest clear spot with a thin line back to it. Dashed lines carry each level across to its arrows.
_Avoid_: Bracket, dimension line

**Level label**:
The words on a level: Reactants, Products, Transition state or Intermediate (numbered when there's more than one), the teacher's own text with formulas typed as `N_2 + 3H_2` as in the key of a particle diagram, a blank line, or nothing.
_Avoid_: Annotation, tag

**Level diagram**:
The second style: no curve, just the reactants and products as horizontal lines with the ΔH arrow between them, for enthalpy change questions.
_Avoid_: Enthalpy diagram (fine in search keywords), energy level diagram (fine in help text), Hess cycle (a different figure)

**Shape only**:
The energy axis with no numbers or gridlines, fitted to the levels, for a diagram where the shape is the point (the enzyme setup). Numbered, with light gridlines, is the default.
_Avoid_: Unnumbered, qualitative

### Bond energy curves

**Bond energy curve**:
A graph of the potential energy of two bonded atoms (up the side, in kJ/mol or kJ mol⁻¹) against the internuclear distance (along the bottom): a curve that comes steeply down a wall, through a lowest point at the bond length and minus the bond energy, and rises toward the zero line. The generator is Bond Energy Curve. Only the bond length and bond energy are data: every curve has the same shape, stretched to its own two numbers (`morse.ts`, a Morse curve with a · r₀ = 3, near most real bonds' value, so it crosses 0 at about 0.77 of the bond length), so the minimum is exact and the width is not. The grid and titles are `$shared/graph`'s, with the axes along the grid's edges as on a heating curve.
_Avoid_: Potential energy diagram (the other figure, against the reaction coordinate), Morse curve, Lennard-Jones (both search words only), potential well diagram

**Internuclear distance**:
The x-axis: how far apart the two nuclei are, a real length, numbered. Kept in pm; the teacher can show it in Å or nm instead, which changes the axis and the labels, not the curve.
_Avoid_: Separation, bond distance (fine in search keywords), reaction coordinate, equilibrium distance

**Bond length**:
The internuclear distance at a curve's lowest point, typed by the teacher, 10 to 1000 pm.
_Avoid_: Equilibrium distance, r₀ (fine in code)

**Bond energy**:
How far below the zero line a curve's lowest point is, typed by the teacher as a positive number, 1 to 2000 kJ/mol. The minimum is at minus the bond energy.
_Avoid_: Bond enthalpy, bond dissociation energy (both fine in help text), well depth (fine in code)

**Bond**:
One curve on the graph: one of up to three, each a name, a bond length and a bond energy the teacher types. The first screen's Bond X, Bond Y and Bond Z are made up, and the page says so. An empty name is "Bond 1".
_Avoid_: Molecule, substance, curve (for what the teacher types)

**Zero line**:
The line across the grid at 0 on the energy axis, the energy of the two atoms far apart, drawn in the axes' ink. Every curve ends under it; the fitted axes always put it on a gridline.
_Avoid_: Baseline, x-axis (the distance axis is along the grid's bottom edge, below the well)

**Well**:
The dip in a curve around its lowest point. A well under two blocks deep is too squashed to read, and the page says so.
_Avoid_: Trough, valley

**Hidden names**:
The bonds' names left off the figure, so its key and its screen-reader description call them Bond A, B and C, for students to match to a table. The answer key names them ("Bond A is Bond X"). Only with two or three bonds: one bond is always named.
_Avoid_: Anonymous, mystery bond

**Guide line**:
A dashed line from a curve's minimum across to the energy axis, or down to the distance axis. Off by default, so students find the minimum themselves. Each can be labeled by its foot with the value ("150 pm", "−300 kJ/mol"), with "Bond length" and "Bond energy", or with a blank line. Where two bonds' guide lines lie along each other, one is drawn, with one label. A length with no room beside its line is written on it, on a white patch; where the labels are crowded anyway, the page says so.
_Avoid_: Drop line, reference line

**Fitted axes**:
What the Fit button gives, and what shape only always uses: the distance axis from 0 to about three times the longest bond length, the energy axis from just below the deepest well to about a quarter of its depth above 0, both in round steps, so the bond length and energy are read from the graph, not from a gridline made for them. Typing a length or energy never moves the axes; the page says when a minimum is off the grid.
_Avoid_: Auto-scale, zoom

### Organic structures

**Organic structure**:
The molecule a teacher draws in the Organic Structure Editor: its carbons and other atoms other than hydrogen, the bonds between them, and the shape they were drawn in. Every view shows the same organic structure in that same shape.
_Avoid_: Structure (that's a Lewis structure's), molecule (that's a particle in a particle diagram), drawing

**Added hydrogen**:
A hydrogen the editor gives an atom to fill its usual valence (C 4, N 3, O 2, S 2, halogens 1; none for R or X, which stand for any group) after its bonds, so the teacher never draws them. A hydrogen the teacher drags in from the tray is a drawn atom and counts toward its neighbor's valence. In the full structural formula the teacher can swing an added hydrogen round its atom in Move; the atom keeps where its hydrogens sit until its number of them changes.
_Avoid_: Implicit hydrogen (fine in help text), auto hydrogen

**View**:
How the organic structure is shown: skeletal formula, carbon skeleton, condensed formula or full structural formula. The teacher can draw in any view, and copying or downloading gives the view showing.
_Avoid_: Mode (that's Move, Draw or Delete), format, style

**Skeletal formula**:
The view with bonds alone: each carbon is a corner or line end with no label, and its hydrogens aren't shown. Other atoms are labeled with their hydrogens (OH, NH₂). A carbon drawn in a straight line between two bonds can't be seen, so the editor points it out. The teacher can label the CH₃ ends (H₃C…CH₃).
_Avoid_: Line diagram, line-angle formula, bond-line formula

**Carbon skeleton**:
The view with a C at every carbon and no hydrogens on carbon; other atoms are labeled with their hydrogens as in the skeletal formula.
_Avoid_: Carbons only

**Condensed formula**:
The view with each carbon labeled with its hydrogens (CH₃, CH₂, CH, C) and the bonds between those groups drawn. A label reads backward (H₃C, HO) when its bond comes from the right, unless the teacher turns that off and every label reads forward (CH₃, OH).
_Avoid_: Condensed structural formula (fine in help text), condensed structural diagram

**Full structural formula**:
The view with every atom and every bond drawn, each added hydrogen on its own bond, O–H and N–H included, set in the open space around its atom.

**Ring labels**:
The C on each ring carbon (with its hydrogens, in the condensed and full structural formulas), shown in every view but the skeletal formula unless the teacher turns them off, so a ring can be a bare polygon as in the skeletal formula while the rest of the organic structure is labeled. Atoms other than carbon in a ring keep their labels.
_Avoid_: Skeletal rings

**Chiral center**:
A carbon the teacher marks by dropping the * card on it, drawn with an asterisk in every view: in its label (*CH, *C), or beside the corner where it has no label. The editor never works out chirality itself.
_Avoid_: Stereocenter (fine in help text), asymmetric carbon

**Acid group**:
A carbon with a double bond to one O and a single bond to another, neither O bonded to anything else. With "Write acids as CO₂H" the condensed formula and carbon skeleton write it as one label, CO₂H (HO₂C facing left); the skeletal and full structural formulas still draw its bonds.
_Avoid_: Carboxyl label, COOH
_Avoid_: Displayed formula, expanded structural formula, Lewis structure (that has lone electrons)

**Carbon numbering**:
Small numbers beside the carbons of one chain or ring in each organic structure, shown in every view. The editor numbers the ring if there is one, otherwise the chain through a C=O (then through a carbon bonded to O or N), longest first, from the end nearest the C=O, then an O or N, then a multiple bond, then a branch (so an acid's or ester's C=O carbon is 1); the teacher can flip it to run the other way. Branch carbons aren't numbered. It isn't IUPAC naming.
_Avoid_: Locants (fine in help text), carbon labels

### Electrochemical cells

**Electrochemical cell**:
A galvanic (voltaic) cell of two metals: each an electrode standing in a 1 M solution of its own ion, in its own beaker, the beakers joined by a salt bridge and the electrodes by a wire through a **meter**. The metal with the lower standard reduction potential is the anode, on whichever side the teacher puts it. The answer key gives the anode, the cathode and E°cell, then the half-reaction at each electrode and the overall reaction, balanced so the electrons cancel. Electrolytic cells, concentrations other than 1 M and cell notation aren't drawn.
_Avoid_: Battery, voltaic pile, half-cell (one beaker of it)

**Meter**:
What the wire passes through between the electrodes: a digital voltmeter, a dial voltmeter (0–4 V) or a plain coil of wire with no reading. A voltmeter's + terminal is wired to the cathode, so it always reads E°cell as a positive number: the table's, or the teacher's own, typed from 0 to 9.99 V and kept to 0.01 V (a dial's needle stops at 4 V).
_Avoid_: Galvanometer, load, resistor
